# Funder prospect research

Good prospecting turns a long list of names into a short list of funders who give to organisations like yours, for work
like yours, in your place, at amounts that make an application worthwhile. Evidence comes from what funders *actually
fund*, not just what their website says.

## 1. Define the profile first

Write a one-paragraph applicant profile: mission, program to fund, geography served, population, budget size, amount
sought, and timeline. Every prospect is scored against this.

## 2. Find candidates

| Source | Use for |
| --- | --- |
| Candid (Foundation Directory) | Searchable funder profiles and grants data (subscription; many libraries offer free access) |
| ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer | Free 990/990-PF filings and financials by EIN |
| IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search | Confirm status, download e-filed returns |
| Grants.gov, SAM.gov assistance listings | US federal opportunities |
| State/local government and community foundation sites | Regional opportunities |
| Peer organisations' annual reports and donor lists | Funders already supporting similar work |
| Corporate social responsibility / foundation pages | Corporate giving priorities and employee-match programs |

Search by: peers' funders, subject area + geography, and community foundations in the service area.

## 3. Read the 990s

- **Private foundations (990-PF)**: total assets, total grants paid, and the supplementary information listing grants
  paid during the year (recipient, amount, purpose) and application information (contact, whether unsolicited
  proposals are accepted, deadlines, restrictions). Look at 2–3 years to see patterns.
- **Public charities (990)** that make grants (e.g. community foundations): Schedule I lists domestic grants over
  $5,000.
- Extract: number of grants, **median** grant size (not the average, which large outliers skew), range, share going to
  your subject area and geography, repeat grantees (high repeat share = harder for newcomers), first-time grantees.
- Note the filing year; 990s lag one to two years. Check the website for newer strategy changes.

## 4. Check fit and eligibility

For each funder record:
- Stated priorities and whether recent grants match them (both must match).
- Geographic restrictions; organisation type restrictions (e.g. no grants to individuals, only 501(c)(3)).
- "Contributions only to preselected organisations" / "does not accept unsolicited requests" — not a direct prospect;
  note any board or staff connections instead.
- Application process: open RFP, LOI first, invitation only; deadlines and cycles.
- Typical grant vs your ask; funders rarely give far above their usual range to new grantees.
- Connections: board members, staff, shared partners (only from public information).

## 5. Score and rank

Score each 0–3 and weight:

| Criterion | Weight |
| --- | --- |
| Mission/program alignment (from actual grants) | 3 |
| Geographic fit | 3 |
| Grant size fit for the ask | 2 |
| Accessibility (open to applications, newcomer share) | 2 |
| Relationship/connection | 1 |
| Timing (deadline fits our capacity) | 1 |

Tier A (strong, pursue now), B (cultivate first), C (monitor). Any hard eligibility failure is excluded regardless of
score.

## 6. Output

```markdown
| Rank | Funder | Type | Typical grant (median, range) | Fit evidence | Process & next deadline | Tier | Next step |
```

Then, per Tier A funder, a short profile: priorities in their words, 3 recent comparable grants (recipient, amount,
year, purpose) with the 990 or website source, contact route, and a suggested angle for the approach.

## Rules

- Cite the source and year for every figure; never estimate giving data you didn't find.
- Use only public information about people; don't compile personal details beyond professional roles.
- Flag stale data (990 older than 3 years, website not updated).
- Respect funders' stated "no unsolicited proposals" — recommend relationship building, not cold submissions.

## Pitfalls

- Matching on keywords on the website while the grants list shows the funder gives almost entirely to universities or
  hospitals.
- Using the average grant size.
- Ignoring that a funder only makes multi-year grants to long-term partners.
- A 200-name list with no ranking; deliver a prioritised top 10–20.
