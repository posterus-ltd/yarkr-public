# Support tone and empathy

Customers judge a reply on three things: did they understand me, are they going to fix it (or tell me straight), and
what happens next. Empathy is shown by specifics, not by apology adjectives.

## Reply structure

1. **Acknowledge the specific situation** in one sentence, using their details.
2. **Own it**: say what you are doing, in first person. Apologise once, for the actual impact, if we caused it.
3. **Answer or act**: the fix, the steps, or the honest status.
4. **Next step with a time**: who does what by when.
5. **Close** without boilerplate.

Example (outage, angry customer):

> Hi Maria,
>
> You've had two days of failed invoice syncs during your month-end close. That's exactly when you need it working,
> and I'm sorry we've caused that.
>
> Our engineers confirmed a bug in the Xero connector affecting accounts with multi-currency enabled, including yours.
> A fix is being tested now. In the meantime, you can export invoices as CSV from Invoices → Export and import them in
> Xero (steps: <link>); I've checked this works for your account.
>
> I'll update you by 15:00 UTC today with the fix's release time, and I'll re-run your failed syncs once it's out.
>
> Tom

## Empathy that works vs that grates

| Grates | Works |
|---|---|
| "We apologise for any inconvenience this may have caused." | "I'm sorry your team couldn't log in this morning." |
| "I understand your frustration." (generic) | "Losing an afternoon's edits is genuinely painful." (specific) |
| "As per our policy..." | "We can't refund partial months, but here's what I can do..." |
| "Unfortunately, that's not possible." | "That isn't something the product does today. The closest option is..." |
| "Please be advised..." / "Kindly note..." | Plain statements |
| "You should have..." | "Next time, turning on X will prevent this." (only if useful) |

## Rules

- **Match their register.** Formal customer, formal reply; short message, short reply. Never mirror anger or sarcasm.
- **Mirror their words** for their problem ("sync", "board", "the red banner"), not internal jargon.
- **Ownership language:** "I'll", "I've checked", "I've asked our billing team". Avoid passive voice that hides who
  acts ("it will be looked into").
- **One apology**, early, for the impact. Don't apologise for things that aren't our fault in a way that admits
  liability; acknowledge instead ("That sounds stressful").
- **Be honest about uncertainty.** "I don't know yet; I'll find out by <time>" beats a guess. Never promise dates,
  refunds or features you haven't confirmed.
- **No blame:** not the customer ("you didn't"), not another team ("engineering broke it"), not "the system".
- **Specific next step with a time** in every reply that doesn't fully resolve the issue.
- **Keep it scannable:** short paragraphs; numbered steps for instructions; bold only for the one thing they must not miss.

## De-escalation for angry or abusive messages

1. Ignore the heat, answer the substance. Pick out the concrete problem(s) and address each.
2. Acknowledge the impact once; don't over-apologise (it reads as scripted).
3. Offer a concrete action or choice, giving back a sense of control.
4. If they threaten to churn, don't bargain in the first reply; solve the problem, then involve the account owner.
5. For abuse (slurs, threats), follow the team's policy: one calm boundary statement, then escalate to a lead.
   Threats of harm go to the lead immediately.

## Delivering bad news

- Lead with the answer; don't bury a "no" in paragraph three.
- Give the reason in one sentence, without hiding behind "policy".
- Offer the best available alternative or what would change the answer.
- See policy-explanation-replies for refusals involving refunds, limits or exceptions.

## Self-check before sending

- [ ] Does the first line show I read *their* message?
- [ ] Is there exactly one apology, and is it for a real impact?
- [ ] Did I answer every question they asked?
- [ ] Is the next step clear, with an owner and time?
- [ ] Could any sentence be read as blaming them?
- [ ] Have I promised anything I can't guarantee?
- [ ] Would this read fine if they forwarded it to their boss?

## Pitfalls

- Exclamation-mark cheerfulness to a frustrated customer ("Great question!").
- Long empathy preambles before the answer; empathy is one sentence, the help is the rest.
- Copy-pasted empathy lines that appear in every reply.
- Over-familiarity (emoji, slang) with customers who haven't used it first.
